\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genomic language models report strong results on benchmarks for viral phenotype prediction. However, benchmark performance alone does not establish whether a model offers capabilities beyond conventional sequence methods, nor does it reveal how much that performance depends on the similarity between viral sequences in the test set and those in the training data. We compared five genomic language models against up to seven conventional predictors across nine viral classification tasks. Neither approach consistently outperforms the other, with their relative performance varying across tasks and evaluation settings. On the published test sets, foundation models outperform conventional predictors on two tasks, conventional predictors lead on three, and four comparisons are inconclusive. The leading conventional method varies across tasks, highlighting the importance of evaluating against multiple baselines. When uncertainty is estimated over biological source units rather than over sequence fragments, only two comparisons remain resolved, and in both a conventional predictor outperforms the foundation model. Conventional predictors can succeed on these tasks by transferring labels from closely related training sequences, and we therefore re-evaluated the same trained predictors on the subset of test sequences with no detectable nucleotide relative in their own supervised training split. Performance declined substantially for foundation models and conventional predictors alike. Across all four substantial filtered populations, the selected foundation model outperforms every available conventional method, which corresponds to the comparison encountered in practice. Its relative advantage does not, however, increase, since holding the comparator set fixed across levels leaves the margin unchanged or reduced on every benchmark except one, so the widening of the reported margin reflects the loss of homology-transfer arms from the comparator rather than improved relative model performance. Repeating the similarity search after six-frame translation shows that most nucleotide-divergent sequences retain a detectable translated relative in the training data, so these results characterize performance on viruses that are divergent at the nucleotide level but whose encoded proteins remain recognizable, rather than performance on evolutionarily novel sequences. A viral benchmark score therefore cannot be interpreted as a capability estimate without a broad comparator set and an explicitly stated level of train--test relatedness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Genomic language models trained by self-supervision on large DNA corpora \citep{nguyen2024evo,brixi2025evo2,dallatorre2024nt,zhou2024dnabert2,nguyen2023hyenadna} are increasingly applied to the prediction of viral phenotypes from genomic sequence. Virus-focused models have reported strong performance on tasks such as host tropism, pathogenicity and transmissibility \citep{dutta2026hvilm,dip2024patholm}. These results have begun to inform discussions of surveillance, model release and safeguards, including proposals to remove hazardous capabilities from open weights \citep{black2026safeguards}. Several of these phenotypes were already being predicted from genome sequence by conventional means before genomic language models were applied to them, human infectivity among them \citep{mollentze2021}. In such discussions, benchmark scores are frequently interpreted as capability estimates. This interpretation requires two elements that a score does not supply on its own. The first is an adequate comparator, because a capability already provided by an inexpensive conventional predictor is not one that the model uniquely contributes. The second is an explicit characterization of the relatedness between test sequences and supervised training sequences, because performance obtained largely on test viruses with closely related labelled counterparts is uninformative about performance when no such counterpart is available.

Neither requirement is routinely met. Tuned supervised models are frequently competitive with genomic foundation models \citep{xu2025specialized,patel2024dart,ledneva2026geneb,phagehost2025}, and transferring the label of the most similar training sequence is a standard and strong predictor in protein function prediction, viral classification and enzyme annotation \citep{kulmanov2020deepgoplus,sanderson2023proteinfer,cafahomology,borozan2015viral,fahmy2026retrieval,sathyamoorthy2026ec}. Biological sequence test sets also retain substantial similarity to their corresponding training data. \citet{petti2023graphpart} present GraphPart, a method designed to partition biological sequence datasets while reducing homology across splits. \citet{spectra2024} study how evaluation design and dataset composition affect estimates of model generalizability for molecular datasets, and \citet{rafi2026hashfrag} examine homology-induced data leakage and memorization in genome-trained sequence models. Because amino acid sequences can remain conserved despite nucleotide substitutions, translated similarity searches can reveal homologous relationships that nucleotide searches fail to detect \citep{perelygin2025host,virhunter2022}.

Viral evaluation suites have begun to address both requirements. \citet{ye2026virobench} benchmark a large set of nucleotide foundation models alongside conventional baselines on viral tasks, and report that performance degrades under taxonomic and temporal shift. Their splits are defined at the level of taxonomic rank and collection date, whereas the approach taken here measures the residual relatedness of each individual test sequence to the benchmark's own supervised training split and rescores the same trained predictors at stated levels of that relatedness.

We therefore benchmark five genomic language models against a broad range of conventional predictors across nine viral classification tasks, assessing the performance of the same trained predictors at three levels of train--test relatedness. We designate these levels \textsc{l0}, \textsc{l1} and \textsc{l2}. \textsc{l0} is the published test set and characterizes how the methods compare under the benchmark as released. \textsc{l1} retains only those test sequences with no detectable nucleotide relative in the supervised training split. \textsc{l2} retains only those with no detectable relative at either the nucleotide or the six-frame-translated amino acid level.

The two filtered levels are motivated by the fact that conventional predictors can succeed on these tasks by transferring labels from closely related training sequences, making this mechanism difficult to distinguish from sequence modelling on the basis of a single score. Every predictor we compare is fit on the benchmark's own supervised training split, which is therefore precisely the set from which any of them could transfer a label. \textsc{l1} retains the test sequences with no detectable nucleotide relative in that set, thereby removing this route simultaneously from the foundation models and from the conventional comparators, and allowing the comparison to be re-examined once it is unavailable. The same reasoning motivates a second level, since nucleotide search is not the only means of identifying a relative. Translated search recovers relationships that nucleotide search misses, so sequences retained at \textsc{l1} may nonetheless remain accessible to conventional homology transfer at the protein level. \textsc{l2} quantifies how many such sequences there are, and serves as a diagnostic that constrains the strongest claim an \textsc{l1} result can support, rather than as a third comparison.

This ordering reflects standard annotation practice, in which a nucleotide reference search is performed first, a translated or profile-based search follows, and a predictive model is applied only when both fail to return a match.

Our principal finding is that neither foundation models nor conventional baselines consistently outperform the other. Under the published conditions, the genomic language models show no general advantage over conventional predictors, the outcome varies across tasks, and three different conventional methods achieve the highest score across the nine benchmarks, so an evaluation that compares against a single baseline will overstate the contribution of the model. When uncertainty is estimated over viruses rather than over sequence fragments, the only comparisons that remain resolved are two in which a conventional predictor outperforms the model. Removing nucleotide-level label transfer substantially reduces the performance of every method, demonstrating the extent to which published performance on both sides depended on that transfer rather than on sequence modelling. The selected foundation model continues to outperform every available conventional method on each filtered population, which is the operationally relevant comparison. Its relative advantage does not, however, increase. The reported margin widens, but a comparison restricted to the comparator arms available at both levels shows the margin unchanged or reduced on every benchmark except VHDB, indicating that the widening reflects the loss of homology-transfer arms from the comparator set rather than improved relative model performance. Foundation models and conventional supervised predictors therefore degrade in parallel, with a single benchmark as an exception on which the model's advantage does increase. Together, these results delineate what current viral benchmarks do and do not establish about genomic language models, which bears directly on evaluations intended to inform release and safeguard decisions.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Benchmarks}

We audit nine viral classification tasks drawn from three sources. Six are drawn from HVUE, the evaluation suite introduced alongside the virus-specialized model HViLM \citep{dutta2026hvilm}. We evaluate the released HVUE v2 splits using their original configurations. They encompass three viral phenotypes, namely human host tropism (whether a virus can infect humans), pathogenicity, and transmissibility. Each phenotype is evaluated under two split configurations, differing in stringency. Only host tropism designates its configurations by sequence identity, \textsc{hard\_70} and \textsc{standard\_95}, where identity denotes the percentage of aligned positions matching between a test and a training sequence, such that the stricter split is intended to impose greater evolutionary distance between train and test. Pathogenicity and transmissibility designate theirs \textsc{hard} and \textsc{standard} without specifying a threshold, and additionally provide a temporal split, which we do not use. In tables and figures we abbreviate \textsc{standard} and \textsc{standard\_95} as \textsc{std} and \textsc{std\_95}.

We independently also developed a novel task based on Virus-Host~DB \citep{mihara2016vhdb}, VHDB. This task addresses human host tropism, but negative examples are drawn from the same viral families as the positive examples, preventing predictors from relying solely on family membership. It is the only benchmark in our study that provides both a genome identifier and a lineage label for every sequence.

Two tasks are drawn from GUE \citep{zhou2024dnabert2}, a general genomic benchmark suite. The first is phage fragment classification, which determines whether a sequence originates from a bacteriophage. The second is SARS-CoV-2 variant classification, the only multiclass classification task in our benchmark. Its released test split contains sequences that also appear in its training split. We evaluate it on the split as released and interpret its result accordingly.

All sequences are divided into 1{,}000\,bp windows. Class balance varies widely across the nine tasks. The positive class comprises 3.5 and 3.6 percent of the two host tropism test sets and 19.1 percent of VHDB, whereas phage classification is balanced at 50.0 percent and the pathogenicity and transmissibility tasks range between 34.6 and 53.1 percent. Test set sizes are reported with each result in Table~\ref{tab:headtohead}.

\subsection{Models}

We evaluate five genomic language models selected to span the design space. Nucleotide Transformer v2 at 500M parameters \citep{dallatorre2024nt} and DNABERT-2 \citep{zhou2024dnabert2} are general-purpose masked language models operating over nucleotide and byte-pair tokens respectively. GENA-LM \citep{fishman2025genalm} extends this family to longer context. HyenaDNA \citep{nguyen2023hyenadna} replaces attention with an implicit long convolution, providing a different inductive bias at comparable scale. HViLM \citep{dutta2026hvilm} is specialized to viral genomes and is the model whose reported viral capabilities motivate this audit. For HViLM we initialized from \texttt{HViLM-base}, a virus-domain continued-pretrained checkpoint, and attached and trained a new sequence classification head using the same supervised training protocol applied to the other foundation models.

Each model is fine-tuned from its pretrained base using a uniform protocol, with learning rates selected via grid search and the optimal epoch determined on the development set. We verify that the selected epoch lies within the training range rather than at its boundary. Performance is reported throughout as the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which remains informative under the pronounced class imbalance of the host tropism tasks. For every benchmark, the model identity and its learning rate were selected by mean development split MCC over three seeds. This selection was pre-committed and held fixed across all three levels and all subsequent runs, so that selection uses no test set quantity and is not revisited when additional fits are added. The development scores reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:selection} are therefore the three-seed quantities that made the selection, not estimates recomputed at the final fit count.

\paragraph{Training repetitions.}
Following selection, the selected model and every stochastic conventional arm were refit ten times with independent seeds on every benchmark and every population, and each method's reported score is the mean MCC across its fits. This covers the two neural baseline arms, namely the convolutional and transformer ladders, and the two stochastic conventional arms, namely the $k$-mer GBT and the amino acid composition GBT. The $k$-mer logistic arm, nearest neighbour lookup and the taxonomy prior are deterministic given the split and carry one fit each by construction. The four foundation models not selected on a given benchmark were not refit. They retain the three fits used for selection, enter no reported interval, and are reported only in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:allarms}.

Two benchmarks fall short of ten fits and we report them as they stand. The VHDB corpus is no longer available to us, so that benchmark retains the fits obtained in its original run throughout, namely three on the model arm, six on each neural ladder and one on each gradient boosted arm. On GUE \textsc{covid} the model arm retains three fits, because the number of epochs used in its original run is not recorded and a refit would not reproduce the selected configuration; its conventional arms carry ten. Every other arm on every other benchmark carries ten. Fit counts and the spread across fits are given per method and population in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:seedsd}. VHDB is also the benchmark carrying the one advantage that survives holding the comparator set fixed, so the weakest-evidenced positive result in the study is the one measured over the fewest fits, and we return to this in the Limitations.

\paragraph{Symmetric selection check.}
As a robustness check we also report a symmetric variant in which the best model and the best conventional predictor are both reselected within each bootstrap replicate. It agrees with the primary analysis on 18 of 20 cells and resolves two further cells, transmissibility \textsc{hard} and VHDB at \textsc{l1}, in the model's favour, so the conclusions reported here are conservative with respect to it. Because reselecting over five models within each replicate both biases the maximum upward and absorbs variance that the pre-committed analysis retains, we do not treat its narrower intervals as evidence of a more secure result.

\subsection{Baselines}

The comparator is the highest-scoring of up to seven conventional predictors on the population under evaluation, each fit on the benchmark's own training split. Not every arm is defined for every benchmark, and nearest neighbour lookup is unavailable outside the published test set, so the maximum is taken over five to seven arms depending on the cell. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:allarms} indicates which arm is defined where.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{$k$-mer logistic} and \textbf{$k$-mer GBT}. Nucleotide 3 to 6-mer frequencies supplied to $L_2$-regularized logistic regression and to gradient boosted trees. These capture base composition and short motif usage.
\item \textbf{Amino acid composition GBT}. Amino acid $k$-mer frequencies of the six-frame translation supplied to gradient boosted trees, capturing composition at the protein level.
\item \textbf{CNN ladder}. A searched supervised convolutional baseline spanning dilated, U-Net and ResNet architectures across approximately two orders of magnitude of capacity, with kernel, dilation, capacity and learning rate swept and the configuration selected on the development split, following \citet{xu2025specialized}.
\item \textbf{Transformer ladder}. A supervised transformer trained from scratch.
\item \textbf{Nearest neighbour lookup}. The label of the top training hit under nucleotide MMseqs2 search \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2}, which quantifies the performance attainable by homology-based label transfer alone.
\item \textbf{Taxonomy prior}. The majority training label of the test sequence's genus, backing off to species and then family, which quantifies the predictive value of lineage membership alone. Genus labels are provided with VHDB. For the two host tropism benchmarks we recover them by MMseqs2 alignment to RefSeq viral and normalize to genus rank through the NCBI taxonomy; this recovered variant is reported in a separate column in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:allarms}.
\end{enumerate}

Each predictor's score is averaged over its fits before the maximum is taken. Nearest neighbour lookup and the taxonomy prior are deterministic given the split and have one fit each.

The comparator arms fall into two groups that behave differently under filtering, and we maintain this distinction throughout the Results. The first five arms listed above are supervised predictors fit on the training split, and they are defined at every level. Nearest neighbour lookup and the taxonomy prior instead transfer a label from a related training sequence. Lookup requires a nucleotide match in the training data, so it applies at \textsc{l0} and is unavailable at \textsc{l1} and \textsc{l2}, which are defined by the absence of such a match, while the taxonomy prior becomes less informative on the filtered populations because lineage membership is less predictive there. Any comparison taking the maximum over all applicable arms therefore compares against a smaller comparator set at \textsc{l1} than at \textsc{l0}. We report both the margin over the best available arm, which corresponds to the situation encountered in practice, and the margin over the five supervised arms alone, which holds the comparator set fixed across levels and thereby distinguishes improved relative model performance from attrition of the comparator set.

\subsection{Three levels of train--test relatedness}
\label{sec:filtering}

The same trained predictors are evaluated on three nested populations of test sequences. \textsc{l0} is the published test set. \textsc{l1} retains the test sequences with no detectable nucleotide relative in the benchmark's own supervised training split. \textsc{l2} retains those with no detectable six-frame-translated relative either. Training data, trained models and the development-selected model and learning rate are identical across all three levels, and only the test population changes. \textsc{l0} and \textsc{l1} constitute comparisons between the foundation models and the conventional predictors. \textsc{l2} is a diagnostic that constrains the interpretation of an \textsc{l1} result, and we report comparisons at that level only where sufficient independent evidence remains.

\paragraph{Search protocol.}
For each test sequence we search the benchmark's own training split using MMseqs2 \texttt{easy-search} \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2} with \texttt{--max-seqs 20}. The nucleotide search uses \texttt{--search-type 3} with \texttt{--min-seq-id 0.30 -c 0.10 --cov-mode 2 -e 1e-3}. The translated six-frame search uses \texttt{--search-type 2 -s 7.5 -e 1e-3}. Filtering is applied to the 1{,}000\,bp windows as distributed by the benchmarks. Profile-based approaches \citep{eddy2011hmmer,steinegger2019hhsuite} and dedicated protein alignment tools \citep{altschul1990blast,buchfink2021diamond} provide complementary opportunities to detect more remote sequence relationships. We did not quantify their additional yield in this study.

\paragraph{Reporting thresholds for filtered populations.}
A population retaining fewer than 20 sequences is not evaluated. A population retaining fewer than 100 sequences, or fewer than 20 examples of its minority class, is evaluated but reported as supporting no summary claim, and such populations are marked in Table~\ref{tab:headtohead}. We refer to the populations clearing both thresholds as substantial. These are reporting conventions, fixed in advance of examining the filtered results. Under the pronounced class imbalance of several benchmarks the binding constraint is the number of minority-class examples rather than the total population size: at the 3.5 percent prevalence of the host tropism test sets, a population of 100 sequences would be expected to contain three or four positives, from which no stable MCC estimate can be obtained. Filtering alters class prevalence as well as population size, so both quantities are evaluated on the filtered population itself rather than inferred from the published test set.

\subsection{Intervals and verdicts}

The reported quantity is the difference in MCC between the selected foundation model and the best conventional predictor on the population under evaluation. A method's score on a population is the mean MCC across its fits, and the comparator is the maximum of that quantity over the applicable arms.

Bootstrap intervals propagate both test sample uncertainty and optimization variability using a two-level bootstrap. Test units are resampled with replacement, and within each method its observed fits are resampled with replacement before the mean MCC is recomputed, with the comparator re-maximized over the applicable arms within every replicate. Deterministic arms contribute no variance at this level. Intervals are 5{,}000-replicate percentile intervals.

We initially applied this scheme with three fits per method and compared it against a conditional scheme that resamples test units only, holding the fit-averaged predictors fixed. The two schemes disagreed on 4 of 20 evaluated populations. Increasing the count to ten eliminated every one of these disagreements. We therefore report the ten-fit primary scheme throughout and interpret the earlier disagreement as a diagnostic of insufficient repetitions rather than as evidence favouring either scheme. Both schemes and both fit counts are reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}.

The test unit resampled at the outer level is either the test sequence or its biological source, and we report both. A sequence-level interval treats each 1{,}000\,bp window as an independent observation, which overstates the available evidence when many windows derive from the same virus. A source-level interval resamples the source units instead, so that all windows from a single virus enter or leave a replicate together. VHDB provides a genome identifier for every sequence, so its source units are native. For the HVUE tasks we recover source units by aligning each window back to RefSeq viral and grouping windows assigned to the same genome. Coverage, defined as the proportion of windows assigned, is reported per cell in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:source}. The two GUE benchmarks provide no usable provenance metadata, so no source-level interval can be constructed for them and they are reported as not estimable at that level.

\paragraph{Dependence among unassigned windows.}
Because a window that cannot be assigned to a genome is treated as its own source unit, the source-level interval assumes those windows are mutually independent. We test that assumption on the one cell where it could matter, namely the one whose source-level interval excludes zero and whose assignment is incomplete, by partitioning the unassigned windows into groups of an assumed size and repeating the source-level bootstrap within each partition (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:hidden}). Assignment to groups is random and carries no claim about which windows share a genome; the procedure fixes only how many dependencies of what size are imposed, and is conservative in that real dependence clusters similar sequences, which a random partition does not. Writing $u$ for the number of unassigned windows and $m$ for the mean size of the assigned groups, four sizes are used: $s=1$, which is the partition the main analysis assumes and leaves every unassigned window independent; $s$ drawn from the empirical size distribution of the assigned groups, which supposes the unassigned windows are grouped as the assigned ones are; $s=m$ and $s=2m$, which impose grouping at the observed mean and at twice it; and $s=u$, a bound in which every unassigned window falls in a single group. Partitioning is performed over the \textsc{l0} rows using the \textsc{l0} assigned-group distribution, so the realised number of \textsc{l1} rows per unit is smaller than the nominal $s$, and the final group of a partition takes the remainder. Scenarios within a partition draw share one shuffle order, so differences between them reflect only the assumed size. Because a single partition is itself arbitrary, each stochastic scenario pools 5{,}000 replicates from each of 20 independent partition draws and we report the standard deviation of the interval endpoints across draws.

A difference is designated \superior{} when the entire interval lies above zero and \inferior{} when it lies below. Any interval spanning zero is designated \inconclusive{}, and we interpret this as an absence of evidence.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Neither model class consistently outperforms the other on the published benchmarks}
\label{sec:performance}

On the published test sets, neither foundation models nor conventional predictors consistently outperform the other, with their relative performance varying by task. Foundation models lead on two of the nine benchmarks, conventional predictors on three, while the remaining four show no clear winner (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}A, Table~\ref{tab:headtohead}).

The two foundation model wins are NT-v2 on phage classification, at $+$0.108~MCC, and HViLM on the stricter host tropism split, at $+$0.111.
The three conventional model wins are a boosted $k$-mer model on SARS-CoV-2 variant classification, at $+$0.022 against the model, nearest neighbour label lookup on the looser host tropism split, at $+$0.072, and a boosted $k$-mer model on transmissibility \textsc{hard}, at $+$0.033.
Several of the four inconclusive comparisons are precise rather than underpowered. On pathogenicity \textsc{std} the difference is $-$0.000 with an interval of $\pm$0.011~MCC, and on transmissibility \textsc{std} it is $+$0.002 with an interval of $\pm$0.012~MCC, indicating that on these tasks the model and the best conventional predictor are not merely indistinguishable but closely equivalent.
The widest of the inconclusive intervals is $\pm$0.04~MCC on VHDB. None of the nine comparisons leaves scope for a large model advantage that the data were insufficiently powered to detect.

Three different conventional methods achieve the highest score across the nine published test sets, so no single baseline is sufficient, although label lookup is the most frequent leader. Label lookup leads on five benchmarks, a boosted $k$-mer model on three and a taxonomy prior on one, and on pathogenicity \textsc{hard} lookup and the convolutional ladder tie at 0.796, which we count as a lookup win. On the looser host tropism split the best supervised conventional arm reaches 0.373~MCC while label lookup reaches 0.619, so a study omitting lookup would have reported a model advantage of $+$0.173 rather than a model deficit of $-$0.072 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:allarms}).

Treating each 1{,}000\,bp window as an independent observation overstates the available evidence, because many windows derive from the same virus. Resampling source units instead alters which results remain supported. Of the five verdicts that resolve at sequence level, two persist at source level, two cannot be estimated because the GUE benchmarks provide no usable provenance metadata, and one becomes inconclusive. Both surviving verdicts are conventional predictor wins: the looser host tropism split at $[-0.140, -0.004]$ and transmissibility \textsc{hard} at $[-0.079, -0.005]$. At the level of biological units, therefore, the only resolved head-to-head comparisons on the published benchmarks are two in which a conventional method outperforms the foundation model. The point estimates themselves are identical under either resampling unit (Table~\ref{tab:headtohead}). We report both schemes and treat a difference as established only where it persists over source units.

\subsection{Removing nucleotide-level label transfer reduces the performance of all methods}
\label{sec:divergent}

A natural objection to the mixed results described above is that conventional predictors appear competitive only because they can transfer labels from closely related training sequences. \textsc{l1} tests this objection directly. We removed the test sequences with a detectable nucleotide relative in their benchmark's own training split and re-evaluated every predictor on the remaining sequences, thereby removing access to a closely related labelled nucleotide match from the foundation models and the conventional comparators simultaneously.

The proportion of each benchmark that survives filtering varies widely. Phage classification retains 2{,}874 of 4{,}630 sequences and VHDB retains 1{,}200 of 3{,}754, whereas the stricter host tropism split retains 11{,}994 of 51{,}187 and SARS-CoV-2 variant classification retains none, because its released test split overlaps its training split entirely. These proportions reflect how the benchmarks were constructed.

Performance declined substantially for both classes of method. On the stricter host tropism split the best conventional predictor falls from 0.62 to 0.10~MCC and the selected foundation model falls from 0.73 to 0.28. On VHDB the conventional predictor falls from 0.68 to 0.18 and the model from 0.70 to 0.50 (Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}). Much of the published performance on these benchmarks therefore depended on the availability of a closely related labelled sequence rather than on sequence modelling by either class of method. We do not claim that relatedness alone accounts for the decline, since the filtered population also differs from the published one in taxonomic composition, class prevalence, sequence composition and the set of applicable comparator arms.

On the filtered populations the selected foundation model outperforms every conventional method defined there. Four nucleotide-divergent populations are substantial by the criteria defined in Methods, and on all four the model scores above the best available conventional predictor, by $+$0.320 on VHDB, $+$0.174 on the stricter host tropism split, $+$0.118 on the looser split and $+$0.117 on phage classification, with every sequence-level interval excluding zero (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}B).
This corresponds to the comparison encountered in practical application. A sequence with no nucleotide relative in the reference data denies a practitioner the same lookup that it denies the benchmark, so the arms removed from the comparator here are arms that would likewise be unavailable in practice.

The relative advantage of the foundation models does not, however, increase once this transfer route is removed. The comparator is a maximum over the applicable arms, two of which transfer a label from a related training sequence, so filtering removes nearest neighbour lookup entirely and weakens the taxonomy prior. Restricting the comparison to the five supervised arms defined at both levels holds the comparator set fixed, and thereby distinguishes improved relative model performance from attrition of the comparator set.
Against this fixed comparator set the margin does not increase on either host tropism split. It falls from $+$0.209 to $+$0.174 on \textsc{hard\_70} and from $+$0.173 to $+$0.118 on \textsc{std\_95}. It is approximately unchanged on phage classification, from $+$0.108 to $+$0.117, and increases only on VHDB, from $+$0.165 to $+$0.463.
The growth in the reported margin, from $+$0.111 to $+$0.174 on \textsc{hard\_70} and from $-$0.072 to $+$0.118 on \textsc{std\_95}, therefore reflects attrition of the comparator set rather than improved relative model performance. VHDB is the exception, and there the taxonomy prior that constitutes the comparator at \textsc{l1} remains available, so the increase is not an artefact of a reduced comparator set.

The foundation models also do not degrade more gradually in absolute terms. On host tropism \textsc{hard\_70} the selected model loses 0.456~MCC between the two levels, exceeding the loss of every one of the five supervised arms, which decline by between 0.076 and 0.454.
The model does retain a larger proportion of its own score, but this follows from its higher starting point rather than from a smaller absolute decline. On \textsc{std\_95} the pattern is mixed, with some supervised arms declining less than the model and others declining more. VHDB is again the exception, where the model loses less than every supervised arm. The accurate summary is therefore that genomic language models and conventional supervised predictors degrade in parallel when nucleotide-level transfer is removed, that the foundation models remain ahead throughout, and that the published benchmarks create the appearance of more graceful degradation because the strongest baseline at \textsc{l0} is a lookup procedure that cannot be applied at \textsc{l1}.

Two factors limit the strength of the conclusions supported by the remaining evidence, and we quantified the influence of each. The first is the resampling unit. Only the stricter host tropism split retains an interval excluding zero when uncertainty is estimated over biological source units rather than over sequence fragments, at $[+0.07, +0.30]$. On VHDB and on the looser host tropism split the source-level interval spans zero, and phage classification admits no source-level interval because GUE provides no usable provenance metadata. The two findings therefore rest on different lines of evidence, and neither is supported by both. The host tropism result that persists under source-level resampling is a level effect whose comparator-matched margin does not increase, while the VHDB result, which constitutes the only genuine increase under a fixed comparator set, has the widest source-level interval in the study at $[-0.06, +0.60]$.

The second factor is which model carries the result, and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:allarms} indicates that it is not the model class as a whole. On host tropism \textsc{hard\_70} at \textsc{l1}, HViLM reaches 0.278 while the next best foundation model reaches 0.096, against a comparator of 0.104. On VHDB at \textsc{l1}, HViLM reaches 0.495 while every other foundation model falls between $-$0.01 and $+$0.03. On these populations the four models not selected perform at or near the level of the conventional arms rather than above them. Phage classification is the single case in which a general-purpose model, NT-v2, remains clearly above the comparator at both levels. Pretraining on DNA in general is therefore not what sustains model performance above the conventional arms on the host tropism and VHDB tasks; virus-specific pretraining distinguishes the one model that does.

\subsection{The remaining advantage reflects nucleotide divergence rather than evolutionary novelty}
\label{sec:translated}

The interpretation that \textsc{l1} supports depends on the extent to which its sequences are in fact separated from the supervised training data, and this determines how the VHDB result and the filtered-level results above may be cited. If the sequences retained at \textsc{l1} had no remaining detectable relationship to the training data, they would constitute evidence that the models generalize to evolutionarily novel viruses, which is the claim that would bear on surveillance of unfamiliar pathogens and on arguments concerning hazardous capability. If they retain more remote relationships, the result characterizes something narrower, namely performance on viruses that are divergent at the nucleotide level but remain related in ways detectable by protein-level search.

Coding DNA can accumulate nucleotide substitutions while the encoded protein changes considerably less. Many substitutions are synonymous, and the encoded protein is typically under stronger functional constraint than the nucleotide sequence itself, so a nucleotide search can fail to detect a relationship that remains readily apparent after translation. This is common in viruses, where nucleotide evolution is frequently rapid while individual proteins or protein domains remain recognizable.

We therefore searched the nucleotide-divergent populations again in six-frame translation against the same training splits, yielding \textsc{l2}. A detectable translated relative is identified for between approximately two thirds and almost all of these sequences, depending on the benchmark (Figure~\ref{fig:filters}). In these benchmarks, removing nucleotide relatives establishes nucleotide divergence rather than evolutionary independence from the supervised training data.

The contribution of the \textsc{l1} analysis is therefore threefold. It establishes that the selected foundation model outperforms every available conventional method on nucleotide-divergent sequences, which is the setting in which such a model would be applied. It demonstrates that the model does not increase its relative advantage in that setting, because foundation models and supervised conventional predictors degrade in parallel. It identifies one benchmark, VHDB, on which the virus-specialized model does separate from every conventional arm, on viruses that are divergent at the nucleotide level but whose encoded proteins remain recognizable. This constitutes an operationally meaningful setting for surveillance and annotation.

Testing generalization to evolutionarily novel sequences would require evaluation at \textsc{l2}, and no benchmark examined here retains sufficient independent evidence to support it. Of the four cells evaluable at that level, one resolves at sequence level and none resolves at source level. The two host tropism sets retain 3{,}737 and 3{,}882 sequences once translated relatives are removed, but only 80 and 79 of these carry the positive label, drawn from seven and six genera respectively, with three quarters and two thirds of each positive class belonging to a single genus, so the diversity of the minority class rather than the size of the population is what limits these cells. On host tropism \textsc{hard\_70} the difference at \textsc{l2} is $+$0.116 with a sequence-level interval of $[+0.024, +0.205]$ and a source-level interval spanning zero, and on \textsc{std\_95} the difference is $+$0.061 with a sequence-level interval of $[-0.037, +0.153]$ (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:source}). VHDB retains 201 sequences carrying eight positives and phage classification retains 86. We report these as point estimates and draw no inference from them. This reflects a limitation of benchmark construction rather than a measurement of model capability.

\section{Discussion}

Across nine viral classification tasks, neither genomic language models nor conventional sequence-based predictors consistently outperform the other, with relative performance varying across experimental settings. Under the published conditions a foundation model leads on two tasks, a conventional predictor leads on three, and the remaining four comparisons are inconclusive, although in several cases the estimate is close to zero with a narrow confidence interval, reflecting precise estimation rather than insufficient power. When uncertainty is estimated over viruses rather than over sequence fragments, only two comparisons remain resolved, and both are conventional predictor wins. Removing nucleotide-level label transfer substantially reduces the performance of every method, demonstrating the extent to which published performance on both sides depended on the availability of a closely related labelled sequence. Across all four substantial filtered populations the selected foundation model outperforms every conventional method defined there, which corresponds to the comparison encountered in practice. Its relative advantage does not, however, increase. Holding the comparator set fixed leaves the margin unchanged or reduced on every benchmark except VHDB, indicating that the widening of the reported margin reflects attrition of homology-transfer arms from the comparator set; on the largest benchmark the model loses more absolute MCC than any of the five supervised arms. VHDB is the exception, where the model separates from every conventional arm under both measures. The summary is therefore that the foundation models remain ahead but do not become more valuable as related sequences are withdrawn.

This has a direct consequence for how benchmark scores should be interpreted. A high score establishes that a model performs a task under the conditions that benchmark represents. It does not establish that the capability is unique to the model, nor that the capability persists when related labelled examples are unavailable. The evidence most relevant to this conclusion concerns the identity of the comparator, in two respects. Three different conventional methods achieve the highest score across the nine tasks, so a study fixing on any single baseline compares against a suboptimal arm on several of them. On the looser host tropism split, omitting nearest neighbour lookup alone would have converted a model deficit into an apparent model advantage of comparable magnitude in the opposite direction.

A second methodological point concerns the number of training repetitions an evaluation requires. We initially constructed our intervals with three fits per method, and under that count the scheme propagating optimization variability disagreed with the scheme conditioning on the observed fits on 4 of 20 evaluated populations. Increasing the count to ten removed every one of these disagreements, and did so in both directions, since three resolved as the conditional scheme had indicated and one resolved against it. The three-fit standard deviations also bore no reliable relationship to the ten-fit values, differing by factors between approximately one half and five with no consistent direction. A variance estimate based on few repetitions is therefore unreliable rather than systematically biased, and cannot be corrected by rescaling. The same failure mode arose within our own pipeline: two code paths implementing what we took to be the same statistic, one averaging over resampled fits and one drawing a single fit per replicate, were found to differ by 0.065~MCC in the width of one interval, a discrepancy that was not apparent at three fits per method and became material at ten. Disagreement between bootstrap schemes should accordingly be interpreted as an indication that the repetition count is too low, rather than as grounds for preferring one scheme over the other.

The third requirement is to state the level of relatedness at which a score was obtained. A single test set conflates two distinct situations, namely the case in which a closely related labelled sequence is available and the case in which it is not. Reporting at \textsc{l0} and \textsc{l1} separates them, and reporting at \textsc{l2} establishes which of the two an \textsc{l1} result belongs to. In our data, the answer at \textsc{l2} is that most nucleotide-divergent sequences retain translated relatives in the supervised training data, so even the single genuine model advantage we identify characterizes nucleotide divergence rather than evolutionary novelty.

We recommend that viral benchmarks report all three levels and interpret them not as a difficulty ladder, but as successive limits of conventional methods. The biological source of each sequence should be preserved so that uncertainty can be estimated across viruses rather than across fragments, since in our data the resampling unit alone determines which results remain supported. Filtering should ideally be applied before genomes are divided into windows, rather than to the windows themselves as the released splits require here. Each resulting population should retain sufficient independent positive and negative examples to support inference, which the benchmarks examined here do not at \textsc{l2}.

The same principle extends beyond viral classification, although the relevant definition of relatedness depends on the task. For protein function prediction, information can transfer through sequence homologs, conserved domains or structural similarity. For variant effect prediction, dependence may arise through the same gene, protein family, structural domain or homologous residue \citep{gurev2026everest}. For microbial phenotype prediction, species, strain, clonal lineage or shared mobile genetic elements may carry the target label. Regulatory genomics may require separation by genomic locus, species, cell type or related regulatory elements. The general question is not whether a test example is unseen, but which biological relationships permit information to transfer from the training data to that example, and evaluation splits should be designed around those relationships.

These distinctions are most consequential when benchmark results are used to assess biological capability or hazard. On these nine tasks, much of the capability the benchmarks measure under published conditions is not unique to the genomic language models, since conventional predictors achieve comparable performance and outperform them where the evidence is strongest, and much of what both classes of method achieve depends on the availability of closely related labelled sequences. Where this is the case, reducing a single model's benchmark score removes nothing from the wider computational toolchain, and a safeguard justified by that score would not reduce the underlying capability. Removing nucleotide-level transfer sharpens this conclusion. The foundation models remain ahead of the conventional methods on the filtered populations, so a capability assessment restricted to nucleotide-divergent sequences would credit them with genuine predictive value. Capability evaluations intended to inform release decisions should therefore report what a model contributes over conventional tools, at an explicitly stated level of relatedness, with sufficient training repetitions to estimate optimization variability, and with uncertainty estimated over biological units.

\subsection*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our source-level analysis is constrained by the provenance information available in the released benchmarks. VHDB provides native genome identifiers, but the HVUE analysis relies on recovered source units. Windows assigned to the same recovered source are grouped together, while unassigned windows remain singletons. Between 40 and 60 percent of windows are unassigned on the host tropism tasks. These intervals are therefore more biologically conservative than fragment-level intervals, but they do not constitute a fully source-resolved analysis. We repeated the source-level bootstrap under four assumptions about how the unassigned windows group, up to a bound in which all of them form a single unit, reducing the unit count from 7{,}223 to 416. The host tropism \textsc{hard\_70} result at \textsc{l1} remains positive under every one, at $[+0.062, +0.407]$ under the bound, and the interval endpoints move by 0.002~MCC or less across independent partition draws (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:hidden}). Source-level inference is not feasible for the GUE benchmarks because suitable provenance information is unavailable.

VHDB is the only benchmark showing an advantage that persists when the comparator set is held fixed, yet it remains the least well-characterized result in our analysis. Its source-level interval is the widest in the study at $[-0.06, +0.60]$, and its positive class at \textsc{l2} falls to eight sequences, so the benchmark exhibiting the clearest separation is also the one supported by the least independent evidence. It is additionally the benchmark measured over the fewest training repetitions, since its corpus is no longer available to us and its arms therefore retain between one and six fits where every other benchmark carries ten. By the argument of the preceding section, a variance estimate from that few repetitions is unreliable in an unknown direction, so the width of the VHDB intervals should not be read as a stable quantity. We report the $+$0.463 comparator-matched margin because it is the clearest instance of a virus-specialized model separating from every conventional arm, and we note that it is the result most in need of independent replication.

The thresholds at which we stop interpreting a filtered population are reporting conventions rather than power calculations. They were fixed in advance and are stated explicitly in Methods, but a different convention would move the boundary between the cells we interpret and those we report as point estimates only.

\textsc{l1} and \textsc{l2} are defined only relative to the supervised benchmark training split. They do not establish that a test virus or its relatives were absent from the pretraining data of any genomic language model, and we make no claim regarding pretraining exposure.
